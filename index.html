Who Stays in the Background?

I chose Panel Three because its title promised a view from where I actually live. Many conversations about global media still look at the Gulf from outside, as a market to enter or a location to film in. “A Regional Outlook,” moderated by Mohammed Ibahrine, turned that gaze around. Its three talks moved from film sets in the UAE to streaming screens in Kuwait and esports teams across MENA.

As a Journalism and Strategic Communication student with a minor in AI and Media, I arrived curious about platforms. I left thinking more about people. Each talk described a new global player arriving in the region, and each one raised the same issue of visibility.

That issue became the question I carried through the afternoon and through this reflection.

Guiding question

Who gets to be visible — and who stays in the background — when global media comes to the Gulf?

Below, I follow that question through each presentation. I then read the panel against the two books shown during the session and close with what it means for my own work.

01 · Labour

From Background to Binary

Kay Dickinson, University of Glasgow — “From Background to Binary: Migrant Extras Amid Labour Market Upheavals”

Glossary
Supply-chain cinema
It is a mode of filmmaking in which a production is split into parts and sent to whichever location offers the cheapest, fastest and most flexible inputs.
Background extra
A background extra is a performer who fills the frame without speaking lines so that a scene looks populated and real.
Digital double
A digital double is a computer-generated replica of a person that can be reused in a scene without that person being on set.

Dickinson’s starting point was her book Supply Chain Cinema (2024). In it, she argues that big-budget films are now assembled the way consumer goods are, following the logic of . Productions travel to sites such as the UAE for tax rebates, strong logistics and a flexible, largely migrant workforce. The finished film looks seamless, but its labour is scattered across contracts and borders.

Her talk carried this argument into the current upheaval in labour markets. Background extras in the Gulf are often migrant workers hired for a day or a week. They were already the least visible people on set. Now AI tools and allow studios to scan, copy or replace them. The move “from background to binary” turns a person into reusable data.

What unsettled me most was her point about education. Dickinson has argued that English-medium branch campuses in the Gulf can train graduates to fit the industry’s needs: flexible, entrepreneurial and comfortable with uncertainty. I study at exactly such a campus. Her critique made me ask what kind of worker my own degree is preparing me to become.

The extra was never the point of the shot, which is exactly why the extra is the easiest person to delete.

My paraphrase of Kay Dickinson’s argument
The question I kept asking

If the crowd can be generated, who is still paid to stand in it, and who decides that a worker’s face is now a file?

Takeaway

AI does not create invisible labour in film; it makes that invisibility permanent.

02 · Representation

From Local Screen to Global Streams

Najat Alsheridah, independent scholar, Kuwait — “From Local Screen to Global Streams: The Kuwaiti Trope and Netflix Globalization”

Glossary
Trope
A trope is a recurring image, character or storyline that audiences recognise quickly and that comes to stand for a whole culture.
Platform globalisation
It is the process by which a streaming service distributes local content worldwide while shaping it to fit its catalogue and audience data.
Zar
The zar is a healing and spirit-possession ritual with dance, practised in Kuwait and across the region, which Alsheridah has studied on screen.

Alsheridah’s research has long focused on women and dance in Kuwaiti cinema, the subject of her doctorate at the University of East London. She has also written about a women’s dance in the series Mohammed Ali Road (2020). Kuwait’s Ministry of Information cut that scene from local television, even though it circulated elsewhere in the region.

Her talk followed Kuwaiti drama into the Netflix era. The platform’s first Kuwaiti series, The Cage (2022), was followed by The Exchange (2023), set around the Kuwait Stock Exchange in 1988. These titles reached audiences that Kuwaiti television never could. Yet a global catalogue rewards what is legible at a glance. Alsheridah asked whether the platform amplifies the Kuwaiti story or condenses it into an exportable .

The tension also runs the other way. A scene censored at home can still stream abroad. Visibility here depends on which gatekeeper a story faces: the state, the algorithm, or both. As a future business journalist, I also noticed that The Exchange turns a financial market into national memory.

Going global can mean being seen by millions, but only in the shape the platform already expects.

My paraphrase of Najat Alsheridah’s argument
The question I kept asking

When a recommendation thumbnail decides what “Kuwaiti” looks like, who is the author of that image?

Takeaway

Reach is not the same as representation.

03 · Organisation

Cultural Collisions in Play

Mohammed Mohammed, with Lobna Hassan, LUT University — “Cultural collisions in play: Navigating tradition and cultural barriers in a MENA esports organization”

Glossary
Esports organisation
An esports organisation is a company that recruits, manages and markets professional gaming teams, much like a sports club.
Cultural collision
A cultural collision is the friction that appears when the norms of a global industry meet local traditions, values and expectations.
Ecosystem
An ecosystem is the network of players, teams, publishers, sponsors, venues and governments that together make an industry function.

Mohammed Mohammed brought an insider’s view, drawn from about a decade in the Egyptian and Saudi game industries. His co-author, Lobna Hassan, researches games, accessibility, gamification and esports at LUT. Together they looked inside a single MENA .

The regional around that organisation is growing quickly, backed by heavy investment and national strategies. Inside it, the picture was less smooth. Global esports brings its own working culture of constant online presence, streaming personas and unusual hours. These norms meet local expectations about family, gender, faith and what counts as a respectable career. As I understood the talk, the organisation negotiates between the two, often informally and person by person.

I recognised this from my own fieldwork. For a photojournalism essay, I photographed an esports tournament at Virtuocity in Doha. Through the lens, the players on stage were the obvious subjects. The coaches, organisers, technicians and families around them were harder to frame, yet they held the event together.

An esports team is not only a roster of players; it is a daily negotiation between a global industry and local life.

My paraphrase of Mohammed Mohammed and Lobna Hassan’s argument
The question I kept asking

Who gets the spotlight on the esports stage, and who keeps the lights on behind it?

Takeaway

Every new industry in the region is also a new negotiation with tradition.

04 · Theory

Reading the Panel Through Theory

Seb Franklin and Ramon Amaro, as shown in the presentation

Glossary
Dysselection
Dysselection is the process by which a system marks certain people as surplus, so that their needs and visibility stop being prioritised.
Assemblage
An assemblage is a combination of human, technical, social and economic parts that acts together, so its effects cannot be traced to one part alone.

The presentation placed two books beside these cases. Together, they explain why visibility is never a neutral outcome of technology.

In The Digitally Disposed (2021), Seb Franklin argues that digital systems do more than process information. They sort populations into those whose lives are counted as valuable and those treated as disposable, a logic he connects to racial capitalism and to what he calls .

In The Black Technical Object (2022), Ramon Amaro argues that machine learning is not neutral code. It inherits the racial history of statistics and classification. It also carries the interests of those who build and fund it, which makes it an rather than a tool.

Read together, the books connect all three talks. Dickinson showed sorting at the level of the body. Alsheridah showed it at the level of the story, where an algorithmic catalogue decides which version of Kuwait travels. Mohammed and Hassan showed the assemblage at the level of the organisation, where tradition, capital and platforms are bargained every day.

05 · Comparison

Three Cases, Three Axes

To test my guiding question, I compared the three cases on the same three axes. Select an axis to see how each case answers it.

Migrant extras

Global studios gain access to cheap, flexible labour through free zones, rebates and short contracts.

Kuwaiti streams

Kuwaiti creators gain access to global audiences, but only through Netflix’s commissioning and catalogue.

MENA esports

Young players gain access to a professional career, often after negotiating with family and community.

Migrant extras

Studios, logistics firms and, increasingly, the AI vendors that own the digital crowd.

Kuwaiti streams

The platform and its audience data abroad, and the state censor at home.

MENA esports

Game publishers, investors and national strategies set the rules; organisations adapt to them.

Migrant extras

Background workers, now at risk of becoming reusable data without a credit or a wage.

Kuwaiti streams

Stories that do not fit the exportable trope, including women’s dance on screen.

MENA esports

Staff, families and women whose work sustains the scene off camera.

The pattern is consistent. In each case, access arrives through a global intermediary, and the people with the least control over that intermediary are the ones who disappear from view.

06 · Conclusion

What I Take Back to My Desk

  1. Visibility is designed. Contracts, catalogues and censors decide who appears before any audience does.
  2. Automation inherits hierarchy. AI tools reproduce the sorting that already exists in labour markets and media industries.
  3. The local is negotiated, not lost. Kuwaiti creators and MENA esports teams are not passive; they bargain with global players every day.

I plan to work as a business and technology journalist in Qatar. My beat will be startups, venture capital and the AI tools that this panel examined from below. Business coverage usually follows the money and the founders who are already visible. This panel taught me to ask, in every deal, who was scanned, cut or left off the roster to make it work.

Studying at a branch campus does not exempt me from Dickinson’s critique; it obliges me to report with it in mind. That is my answer to the guiding question. Global media will keep deciding who stands in the foreground. The journalist’s job is to widen the frame.
